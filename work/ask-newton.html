Ask Newton: an AI tutor that gives hints before answers

Learners get stuck in the middle of a lecture and lose momentum waiting for help. I designed an in-player AI tutor that answers from the lecture itself, nudges before it tells, and knows when to hand off to a human.

Self-initiated concept. I don't work at Newton School. I made this to show how I'd approach an AI feature for an online learning platform. The personas are proto-personas (informed assumptions I'd check with real learners first), and the test plan below is ready to run.
RoleSolo: research, design, prototype
TimelineNovember 2026
PlatformWeb LMS · lecture player
ToolsFigma · Claude Code · HTML/CSS/JS
Problem

A doubt in the middle of a lecture leaves three bad options: wait for a TA, rewatch and hope, or move on confused. All three kill momentum.

Solution

A side-panel tutor grounded in the exact lecture moment. It gives hints first, offers a direct answer on request, and hands off to a TA with full context.

Output

A working prototype (below), a metrics plan with guardrails, an analytics event spec and a usability test plan.

01 · Problem

Getting stuck is normal. Staying stuck is the problem.

Online learners do most of their learning alone, often late at night after work or college. When a concept doesn't click halfway through a lecture, their options are poor:

Option A

Post in the forum

Wait hours for a reply. By then the context, and the motivation, are gone.

Option B

Rewatch and hope

Same explanation, same confusion. Often ends in skipping ahead.

Option C

Ask a general chatbot

Fast, but it doesn't know how this course teaches the idea, and it hands over the answer without the understanding.

Each of these breaks momentum, and lost momentum is how courses get abandoned. For a platform whose promise is a job at the end, an abandoned module isn't just a lost lecture. It's a step away from the offer.

How might we help a stuck learner get unstuck in under two minutes, without simply handing them the answer?

Why not just add a chatbot?

Learners already paste doubts into ChatGPT. The opportunity isn't another chat box. It's an assistant that knows which lecture you're in, which second you're at, and how this instructor explains it, and that cares about whether you learned, not just whether you got an answer.

02 · Who it's for

Three people, one moment.

Two learners who get stuck, and the TA who currently absorbs every doubt. These are proto-personas: my starting assumptions, written down so research can prove them wrong.

R

Riya, 21 · final-year student, preparing for placements

"I don't want the answer. I want to know why I was wrong."

  • Watches lectures at 1.25× late at night; edge cases trip her up
  • Needs: understanding she can repeat in an interview
A

Arjun, 27 · operations executive switching to tech

"By the time a TA replies, I've forgotten what I was asking."

  • Studies 9–11 PM after work; gets anxious about falling behind his cohort
  • Needs: a fast unblock, and the option to just see the answer when he's tired
M

Meera · teaching assistant

"Half my queue is the same five doubts, with no context."

  • Spends her first reply asking "which lecture? which part?"
  • Needs: fewer repeat questions, and escalations that arrive with context

04 · Design principles

Four rules the design had to obey.

01

Grounded, not generic

Every answer cites the lecture moment it came from. One tap jumps the video there.

02

Hint before answer

Learning sticks when the learner takes the last step. But never trap them: the full answer is always one tap away.

03

Know its limits

When it isn't confident, it says so and routes to a human, with the context already attached.

04

Never break flow

A side panel, not a new page. The video pauses while you ask and resumes where you left off.

05 · Tradeoffs

The decision log

The decisions that shaped the design, what I gave up for each, and why.

✕ Considered

Chat overlay on the video

Feels immersive and uses the full width.

✓ Chose

Side panel next to the player

Video and code stay visible while you ask.

Why: learners need to look at the exact line they're confused about while asking. An overlay hides the very thing they're asking about. Cost: less room on small screens, so on mobile the panel becomes a bottom sheet at 60% height.
✕ Considered

Always answer directly

Fastest path to "unblocked." It's what general chatbots do.

✓ Chose

Hints by default, "Just answer" toggle

Learners choose, and the choice is remembered.

Why: working through a problem helps learners remember it, but forcing that at 11 PM makes people quit. Riya wants hints and Arjun sometimes just wants the answer. Safeguard: the "hint frustration" guardrail tells us if the default is wrong.
✕ Considered

A spinner, then the answer

Simplest to build.

✓ Chose

Show the retrieval step

"Searching Lecture 14 transcript…", then the answer with its source.

Why: from building Lexi I know retrieval adds about a second of delay. When the wait shows what's happening, it feels purposeful, and it sets up the citation. Engineering note: cache answers to each lecture's most common doubts to cut both delay and cost.
✕ Considered

Empty chat box

Maximum freedom.

✓ Chose

Suggested doubts from this timestamp

Chips drawn from what other learners asked at this moment.

Why: stuck learners often can't put the doubt into words. Seeing it phrased by someone else is a relief. Bonus: the red "doubt hotspot" marker on the scrubber tells instructors exactly where a lecture confuses people.

07 · Testing plan

How I'll find out where I'm wrong.

A prototype is just a hypothesis you can click. Here's the moderated test I'd run next, with the result that would make me change course written down in advance.

Who

5 learners

3 students preparing for placements and 2 working professionals. 30 minutes each, remote, think-aloud.

Measure

Success, time, ease

Task success, time on task, hesitations, a one-question ease rating per task, and "Would you trust this answer before an exam?" (1–5).

Tasks

  1. "You're confused by the line at 18:40. Get unstuck."
  2. "Show me where in the lecture that answer came from."
  3. "Ask about something this lecture doesn't cover." (Does the hand-off feel like help or like a dead end?)
  4. "You're tired and just want the answer. Get it."

If more than half of participants tap "Just answer" before reading the first hint, hint-first is the wrong default, and I'll change it.

After launch: the event spec

The prototype already fires the events we'd need, so analytics is part of the design rather than an afterthought.

08 · Learnings & next steps

Designing AI is mostly designing for when it's wrong.

The happy path took an afternoon. Most of the work went into the edges: low confidence, off-topic doubts, the learner who's had enough of hints. My data science background helped most here. I know how retrieval fails, so I could design for those failures instead of being surprised by them.

What I'd do next

  • Instructor view: a doubt heatmap per lecture, so content teams can fix the explanation rather than answer the same doubt 300 times.
  • Hinglish-first: many learners think in Hinglish. The prototype already understands "mid + 1 kyu kiya?" (try it), and the next step is replying in kind.
  • Mobile bottom sheet: design and test the 60% sheet for learners who study on their phones while commuting.
  • Memory: "You asked about loop bounds last week too." Help learners spot their own patterns.
