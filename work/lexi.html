Lexi: designing trust into a legal AI answer engine

I built a retrieval-augmented Q&A system for the Indian Constitution for Primus Partners. The engineering was hard, but the decisions that mattered most were about people: how do you make an AI answer something a lawyer can actually rely on?

Real project, two parts. Sections 1–4 cover what I built and shipped (retrieval, backend, a Streamlit interface). Section 5 is a redesign concept: how I'd design the interface now, looking at it as a product designer.
RoleBuilt end to end: retrieval, API, UI

Problem

A legal answer is useless if you can't verify it, and dangerous if it cites the wrong article with confidence.

People search legal text two ways: by exact reference ("Article 21A") and by concept ("right to education"). The system had to handle both.

A deployed hybrid-search Q&A system, plus a redesign that puts citations, ambiguity and uncertainty up front.

01 · Problem

In law, a confident wrong answer is worse than none.

The Indian Constitution is long, heavily amended and full of cross-references. People who work with it need fast answers, but they also need to prove every answer by pointing at the exact text.

Large language models are fluent, and that fluency is the danger. A model that confidently cites "Article 21" when the answer is actually in "Article 21A" doesn't just fail. It misleads someone who may act on it.

The job wasn't "answer questions." It was answer questions in a way people can check.

02 · The insight

Two ways of asking, one search box.

Looking at how people phrase legal questions, two very different behaviours showed up:

Reference search

"What does Article 243ZH say?"

They know exactly what they want. Precision is everything: 21 and 21A are completely different rights.

Concept search

"Can the state restrict free speech?"

They know the idea but not the article. The system has to understand meaning, not just match words.

Keyword search (BM25) is great at the first and poor at the second. Semantic search is the reverse. So the system uses both. A product decision drove that architecture.

The numbering problem

Constitutional articles don't count neatly. Amendments insert articles like 21A, 31B and 243ZH, and clauses nest like 19(1)(a). Off-the-shelf tokenisers split or mangle these, so "21A" ends up matching "21". I wrote custom logic to recognise and normalise article references before retrieval, so an exact reference always finds its exact article.

04 · What shipped

Working, deployed, and not yet designed.

I shipped the system as a FastAPI service with a Streamlit front end, packaged in Docker so it runs the same way anywhere. Streamlit was the right call for speed, and it let the team try real questions within days.

But looking back with a designer's eye, the interface treated the answer as the product. The sources, which are the reason anyone would trust it, sat below the fold as plain text.

05 · Redesign concept

Before and after: make the evidence the interface.

  1. Ask about ambiguity, don't guess. "Article 21 education" could mean two things. The system asks in one tap instead of silently choosing one.
  2. Inline citations on every claim. Each sentence links to its source, so the user can verify any part of the answer.
  3. Quote the exact text. Source cards show the article's actual wording, so users verify against the law itself and not against the AI's summary of it.
  4. Fit into real work. "Copy with citations" pastes an answer with its references straight into a brief or memo, which is what users do next.

The state that matters most: "I don't know"

When retrieval finds nothing relevant, V2 says so: "I couldn't find this in the Constitution. It may be covered by a statute or case law instead." It never generates an answer from the model's general knowledge. In legal work, a graceful refusal builds more trust than a fluent guess.

06 · Trust principles

What I now bring to every AI feature.

Lexi taught me principles I've since applied to designing Ask Newton for learners:

01

Show your sources

Every answer points to where it came from. In Ask Newton, that's a lecture timestamp.

02

Make uncertainty visible

Ambiguity and low confidence are states to design for, not to hide.

03

Verification in one click

If checking takes effort, nobody checks. Then trust is blind.

04

Refuse gracefully

"I don't know, but here's who can help" beats a confident guess every time.

07 · Learnings

Architecture is UX.

The most important "design" decision in Lexi was choosing hybrid search, and that came from watching how people ask questions. It made me realise I'd been doing design all along, just one layer down. Knowing how AI systems fail (retrieval misses, tokenisation quirks, overconfident generation) lets me design interfaces that are honest about those failures.

If I did it again

  • Sit with 3–4 actual users in week one, not after the system works.
  • Design the source cards before writing the answer-generation prompt. The interface should shape what the AI is asked to produce.
  • Log which citations users open, a cheap and powerful signal of where trust is low.
